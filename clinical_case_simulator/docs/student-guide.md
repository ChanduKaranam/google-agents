# Clinical Case Simulator — Student Guide

## What is it?

A pretend patient you can talk to.

You are given a real-feeling clinical case. You ask the patient questions, examine
them, order tests, work out what is wrong, and then get a report on how well you
thought it through.

It is practice. Nobody is watching, nothing goes on your record, and you can do
the same case again tomorrow.

## Why use it?

On the wards you might see one chest-pain patient a month. Here you can see six
before lunch.

More importantly: it marks **how you got there**, not just whether you guessed
right. Two students can both say "pneumonia" and get very different scores — the
one who asked about the sputum, checked the oxygen and ruled out TB scores far
higher than the one who guessed from the word "cough".

## How a case runs

```
1. Pick a case      →  "Start a beginner case"
2. Take a history   →  ask the patient anything, in your own words
3. Examine          →  "check the vitals", "listen to the chest"
4. Order tests      →  "chest X-ray and a blood count"
5. Give your list   →  your top 3 diagnoses, and WHY for each
6. Say how you'd    →  which test or question separates them?
   tell them apart
7. Commit           →  your final diagnosis and your reasoning
8. Get your report  →  scores, what you missed, what to revise
```

You can go back and forth. Order a test, ask another question, examine again —
just like real life.

## The one rule that surprises everyone

**It will not tell you the answer.**

Ask "is this a heart attack?" and it will ask you what makes you think so. That
is deliberate. The moment it tells you, you stop thinking, and the practice is
worthless.

The patient will not help you either. They only answer what you actually ask.
They will not volunteer the crucial symptom. They speak like a normal person —
if you say "any diaphoresis?" they will look at you blankly, because real
patients do too.

## If you are stuck

Ask for a hint. There are three, each more direct:

1. Points you at a part of the case — *"compare the pulse rate with the temperature"*
2. Names the pattern — *"what makes dullness and bronchial breathing together?"*
3. Names the topic to go and read

Each hint costs you a few marks. That is fine — a hint plus a good answer beats
giving up. You can also ask it to just reveal the answer, but that caps your
score, so try the hints first.

## How you are marked

Six things, out of 100:

| Skill | What it is really asking |
|---|---|
| History taking | Did you ask the questions that mattered? |
| Communication | Did you talk like a doctor talks to a person? |
| Clinical reasoning | Did you use the clues, or just pattern-match? |
| Differential diagnosis | Did you list the dangerous things, not just the likely one? |
| Investigation selection | Did you choose tests, or order everything? |
| Case synthesis | Did you pull it together into one answer? |

The final diagnosis is only 10% of your mark. History, reasoning and
investigation choice are 60% between them.

**So a careful wrong answer scores better than a lucky right one.** That is on
purpose.

## Things that lose marks (and why)

- **Only closed questions.** "Does it hurt?" "Yes." You learn nothing. Open with
  "tell me about the pain."
- **Medical words at the bedside.** The patient does not know what "radiation"
  or "orthopnoea" means. Neither would your uncle.
- **A diagnosis list with no reasons.** Three names is a guess list. Three names
  with reasons is a differential.
- **Ordering every test.** That is not selection. Real patients wait, pay, and
  get harmed by unnecessary tests.
- **Forgetting the dangerous diagnosis.** Chest pain is not just heart attack —
  what about a dissection? You get marks for naming what you ruled out.

## Things that gain marks

- Asking what makes the pain better or worse
- Checking vital signs before anything else
- Asking a family member when the patient cannot tell you
- Saying "I would order X, and if it showed Y, that would point to Z"
- Explaining what you excluded and why

## A worked example

> **You:** What brings you in today?
> **Patient:** I've been having this pain in my chest since this morning.
>
> **You:** Where exactly is it?
> **Patient:** Mostly in the middle of my chest.
>
> **You:** Does anything make it worse?
> **Patient:** It gets worse when I'm walking.
>
> **You:** Does it go anywhere else?
> **Patient:** Sometimes into my left arm.

Notice what happened: four questions, and you now have a story that sounds
cardiac. The patient never said "cardiac". You did that.

## The cases

Eight cases in Internal Medicine, at four levels:

- **Level 1** — obvious clues, one likely answer. Start here.
- **Level 2** — several possibilities, you need the right history and tests.
- **Level 3** — the information conflicts. One case has a patient who tells you
  she is having a panic attack. She is not.
- **Level 4** — the patient cannot give you a proper history at all, and the
  case changes while you are working on it.

Do a Level 1 first even if you feel ready for harder ones. It teaches you how
the simulator expects you to talk to it.

## Honest limits

- The cases were written for training and **have not yet been signed off by a
  consultant**. Treat the teaching points as revision prompts, not as gospel.
- Communication marking is rough. It can spot jargon and closed questions; it
  cannot tell whether you were genuinely kind. Use that score to start a
  conversation, not to settle one.
- **This is a simulation.** It is not a diagnostic tool, and nothing it says is
  advice about a real person. If something in a case worries you about a real
  patient — or about yourself — talk to an actual doctor.

## Getting the most out of it

1. Do the case properly before looking at the report.
2. Read "What you missed" carefully. That is the actual lesson.
3. **Do the same case again a week later.** You will be shocked how much you
   still miss.
4. Do a case with a friend and argue about the differential before submitting.
